# Clinician Review Sample Cases

Status: draft sample for volunteer nurse and clinician review.

Purpose: this document shows how generated Karibu Learn cases can be circulated for clinical sign-off. The app experience will walk learners through cases in real time, but clinician reviewers need a readable narrative version that shows the patient story, key clinical facts, expected reasoning, and review questions.

Reviewer instructions:

- Read each case as if it arrived at an HC III.
- Mark anything that feels clinically wrong, unrealistic, unsafe, outside scope, or poorly worded.
- Confirm whether the expected action matches your understanding of Ugandan clinical practice and Ministry of Health guidance.
- Add practical language you would use with patients or caregivers.
- These are simulated cases only. Do not add real patient names or identifiable details.

## Case 1: Fever With Malaria Triage

Case ID: `hc3-fever-malaria-triage-001`

Chapter: Fever, Malaria, and Acute Illness

Source anchor: Uganda Clinical Guidelines 2023, malaria assessment and treatment.

It is a busy morning at a rural HC III. A 24-year-old woman joins the outpatient queue with fever and headache that have been coming and going for three days. She looks tired and uncomfortable, but she is alert, able to speak clearly, and able to drink.

At triage her temperature is 38.8 C, pulse is 104 bpm, respiratory rate is 20/min, and blood pressure is 112/70 mmHg. She weighs 54 kg. She reports no convulsions, no confusion, no persistent vomiting, and no inability to drink. She does not report cough, pain on urination, neck stiffness, abdominal pain, or vaginal bleeding. Her last menstrual period was two weeks ago and she says there is no possibility of pregnancy. She has no known drug allergy. On examination she is alert, has no jaundice, and has no respiratory distress.

The learner should not jump straight to malaria treatment just because fever is common. The first task is to screen for danger signs: convulsions, altered mental status, inability to drink, persistent vomiting, and respiratory distress. Because no severe features are present, the learner should use the available malaria RDT and consider other causes of fever if the test or presentation does not fit malaria.

In this scenario, the malaria RDT is positive. With fever, positive RDT, ability to drink, normal mental status, no respiratory distress, no persistent vomiting, and no pregnancy, the case should be treated as uncomplicated malaria at HC III. If artemether-lumefantrine is the available first-line ACT and there is no contraindication, the expected treatment is the adult/greater-than-35-kg course: 4 tablets twice daily for 3 days, taken with food or milk if possible. Give paracetamol for fever if needed, encourage fluids, and explain exactly when to return.

If the RDT were negative, the expected action would change: do not give antimalarials automatically; reassess for other fever causes and danger signs, then manage or refer based on findings. That negative-test branch should become a separate case or Level 3 variant, not be blurred into this one.

Expected learner decision:

- Check danger signs before outpatient treatment.
- Use malaria RDT before antimalarial treatment.
- Recognize this exact case as uncomplicated malaria after the positive RDT and absent danger signs.
- Treat with the correct adult ACT course if artemether-lumefantrine is the local first-line option and no contraindication is present.
- Give fever care, hydration advice, adherence counseling, and clear return precautions.
- Refer urgently if severe malaria features or other danger signs are present.

Reviewer questions:

- Is this a realistic HC III fever presentation?
- Are the danger signs complete and worded correctly?
- Is the adult artemether-lumefantrine course correct for current Uganda guidance and local stock?
- Would you ask or document any additional pregnancy or medicine-safety question before treatment?
- What counseling would you give before sending this patient home?

## Case 2: Child With Diarrhoea and Dehydration Assessment

Case ID: `hc3-child-diarrhoea-dehydration-001`

Chapter: Child Health and IMNCI Reasoning

Source anchor: IMNCI diarrhoea dehydration classification; Uganda Clinical Guidelines 2023.

An 18-month-old boy is brought to the HC III by his mother because he has had watery diarrhoea for two days. The mother says he is drinking less than usual and has been more irritable. She has not seen blood in the stool. There have been no convulsions and no cough.

At assessment his weight is 10 kg, temperature is 37.8 C, pulse is 126 bpm, and respiratory rate is 30/min. He is restless and irritable. His eyes look sunken. When offered fluid he drinks eagerly. Skin pinch returns slowly.

This case is meant to test whether the learner classifies dehydration before selecting treatment. The learner should not simply prescribe medicine for diarrhoea. The key task is to check general danger signs, ask about blood in stool, assess drinking ability, look at the eyes, observe thirst, and assess skin pinch.

Given the findings in this scenario, the child has diarrhoea with some dehydration, not severe dehydration: he is restless/irritable, has sunken eyes, drinks eagerly, and skin pinch returns slowly, but he is not lethargic or unconscious, is not unable to drink, and has no signs of shock. The expected management is Plan B oral rehydration at the facility: give ORS 75 ml/kg over 4 hours. At 10 kg, that is 750 ml ORS over the first 4 hours, with small frequent sips and continued breastfeeding/feeding as tolerated. Reassess after 4 hours and reclassify hydration status.

Because he is 18 months old, zinc should be given at the over-6-month dose: 20 mg once daily for 10-14 days. The caregiver should be shown how to mix and give ORS, told to continue feeding, and told to return immediately if the child becomes unable to drink, vomits everything, becomes lethargic, develops blood in stool, develops worsening fever, or has worsening diarrhoea.

Urgent referral or higher-level management is needed if severe dehydration appears: lethargic or unconscious, unable to drink or drinking poorly, very slow skin pinch, shock, or other danger signs. This case has no blood in stool; if blood were present, that should be written as a separate dysentery case with its own specific treatment plan.

Expected learner decision:

- Classify this child as some dehydration based on the listed signs.
- Do not miss severe dehydration or general danger signs.
- Start Plan B: 750 ml ORS over 4 hours for a 10 kg child.
- Give zinc 20 mg daily for 10-14 days for a child older than 6 months.
- Reassess after 4 hours and counsel the caregiver on feeding, ORS, zinc, and return precautions.

Reviewer questions:

- Do these signs match how you would classify dehydration?
- Is the case realistic for an HC III outpatient setting?
- Is the ORS volume and zinc dose correct for your current practice?
- What wording would you use when counseling the mother?
- Are any danger signs missing or overstated?

## Case 3: ANC Visit With High Blood Pressure

Case ID: `hc3-anc-hypertension-danger-signs-001`

Chapter: Maternal Health and Midwifery Emergencies

Source anchor: Uganda Clinical Guidelines 2023, hypertension in pregnancy and referral.

A 28-year-old pregnant woman attends ANC at about 32 weeks by dates. She came expecting a routine visit but mentions that she has had a headache since morning. When asked more directly, she also reports blurred vision. She has no fever, no vaginal bleeding, and no convulsions.

Her first blood pressure is 156/102 mmHg. After she rests and the cuff is checked, repeat blood pressure is 158/104 mmHg. Pulse is 96 bpm, respiratory rate is 18/min, and temperature is 36.9 C. She is alert and not convulsing. There is mild pedal oedema. Urine dipstick at the HC III is 2+ protein.

The learner should recognize that this is not a routine ANC complaint. At 32 weeks, repeated hypertension with headache, blurred vision, and proteinuria is pre-eclampsia until proven otherwise. Even though the systolic BP has not reached 160 mmHg in this draft scenario, the symptoms and proteinuria make same-day escalation necessary. She should not be reassured, sent home, or told to wait for the next ANC visit.

The working diagnosis is pre-eclampsia requiring urgent referral for higher-level maternal assessment and management. At HC III, the expected action is to keep her under observation, inform the maternity in-charge/clinician, arrange urgent referral, and document maternal danger signs clearly. Check fetal movement and fetal heart if available, but do not let that delay referral.

The referral note should include gestational age, both BP readings, headache, blurred vision, urine protein 2+, oedema, absence of convulsions, fetal assessment if done, actions taken, and reason for referral. If local protocol says to give magnesium sulfate or antihypertensive treatment before transfer for this symptom pattern, that should be named in the final version after clinician review. If local protocol reserves magnesium sulfate for severe pre-eclampsia/eclampsia only, that should also be explicit. This is a protocol-specific review point, not a reason to leave the case vague.

Expected learner decision:

- Repeat and confirm the high BP correctly.
- Identify this exact scenario as pre-eclampsia concern: 32 weeks, repeated BP around 158/104, headache, blurred vision, urine protein 2+.
- Keep the patient under observation and arrange same-day urgent referral.
- Communicate clearly with the receiving facility.
- Follow the facility's pre-referral maternal emergency protocol and document what was given or not given.

Reviewer questions:

- Is the referral threshold correct for HC III practice?
- For this exact BP/symptom/proteinuria scenario, should magnesium sulfate or antihypertensive pre-referral treatment be included at HC III?
- Is the BP level and symptom combination realistic?
- What should be included in the referral communication?

## Case 4: Adult Cough Requiring TB Screen

Case ID: `hc3-adult-cough-tb-screen-001`

Chapter: HIV, TB, STI, and Continuity of Care

Source anchor: Uganda HIV/AIDS Consolidated Guidelines 2023, TB symptom screening and HIV/TB integration.

A 42-year-old man comes to the HC III because of cough. At first he says he has "just cough" and asks for medicine so he can return to work. When the clinician asks about duration, he says the cough has lasted three weeks. He has also had night sweats and recent weight loss. He does not initially mention smoking history unless asked. He reports no severe shortness of breath at rest and no haemoptysis.

His temperature is 37.6 C, pulse is 92 bpm, respiratory rate is 22/min, and blood pressure is 118/76 mmHg. He is thin but able to speak in full sentences. There is no chest indrawing.

The learner should recognize that cough duration changes the pathway. A short viral cough and a cough lasting three weeks are not managed the same way. In this exact scenario, cough for three weeks plus night sweats plus weight loss makes him a presumptive TB patient. He should be moved out of a crowded indoor queue if possible, given cough etiquette instructions, and assessed in a well-ventilated area while staff use appropriate IPC.

The working diagnosis is presumptive pulmonary TB requiring diagnostic evaluation. Differentials include pulmonary TB, chronic bronchitis/COPD, pneumonia, and viral cough, but the TB pathway should start today. In this review scenario, the HC III collects sputum and sends samples to the district GeneXpert site through the routine sample transport system. The expected action is to collect sputum today, complete the TB request documentation, give the patient a clear return date for results, and record a working phone contact. HIV testing should be offered today if status is unknown, with consent and privacy.

Smoking history matters for chronic cough counseling, but it must not downgrade the TB concern. The patient should not be given only cough syrup or antibiotics and sent away. He should not be started casually on TB treatment without following the diagnostic pathway, unless directed by national protocol. If severe respiratory distress, haemoptysis, very ill appearance, or inability to eat or drink is present, urgent referral is required. In this case those danger signs are absent, so the key is same-day TB testing/referral, HIV/TB integration, clear follow-up instructions, and documentation.

Expected learner decision:

- Identify this as presumptive TB because cough is three weeks with night sweats and weight loss.
- Use cough etiquette and ventilation rather than leaving him coughing in a crowded room.
- Collect sputum today for district GeneXpert testing through sample transport.
- Offer HIV testing if status is unknown, with consent and privacy.
- Give clear follow-up instructions and safety-net advice.

Reviewer questions:

- Is the cough duration threshold and symptom pattern correct?
- Should HIV testing be framed differently?
- What is the most realistic HC III pathway in your setting: sputum collected onsite, referral to a hub, or sample transport?
- Is the caution about not starting TB treatment appropriate?
- What follow-up instructions would be practical in your facility?

## Case 5: Fever, Bleeding, and Travel History During Ebola Alert

Case ID: `hc3-ebola-fever-bleeding-travel-001`

Chapter: Outbreak, IPC, Ebola, and Viral Haemorrhagic Fever

Source anchor: 2026 Ebola/Bundibugyo outbreak guidance source registered in the Karibu medical corpus; Uganda Clinical Guidelines 2023.

During an Ebola alert, a 31-year-old man arrives at the HC III screening point before entering the outpatient waiting area. The screener asks the outbreak questions immediately because every febrile patient is being screened before registration. He reports fever, severe weakness, vomiting overnight, and bleeding from the gums that morning. He returned four days ago from a district with reported Ebola cases and attended the burial of a relative who died after fever and bleeding.

His temperature is 39.1 C, pulse is 118 bpm, respiratory rate is 24/min, and blood pressure is 98/64 mmHg. These vitals are enough to show he is ill, but the decision does not wait for a full routine examination. Fever plus unexplained bleeding plus travel/contact exposure during a known outbreak meets the alert/suspected-case logic. The learner should not sit with this patient for a normal consultation, palpate the abdomen, send him to the lab queue, or keep him in the waiting area.

The correct first action is immediate IPC and notification. Keep distance, avoid direct contact, give the patient a mask if tolerated, move him by the safest route to the designated isolation/holding area, restrict staff contact to the minimum trained team, use the facility's Ebola PPE process, and notify the district/outbreak response team according to the current Ministry of Health pathway. Record the names of staff or attendants who already had close contact, but do not create panic or announce details publicly.

The working diagnosis is suspected Ebola or viral haemorrhagic fever requiring isolation and notification. Malaria, typhoid, sepsis, and other febrile illnesses remain possible, but they do not come first operationally because the outbreak-risk criteria are met. Testing and transfer should be coordinated through the outbreak response pathway, not routine outpatient lab flow.

This case should be reviewed especially carefully by clinicians and outbreak-response staff. The final app version should use current Uganda Ministry of Health wording for screening questions, notification pathway, PPE expectations, isolation area setup, waste handling, safe transport, and cleaning after patient movement.

Expected learner decision:

- Recognize this exact patient as a suspected Ebola/VHF case at screening, before registration or normal consultation.
- Separate safely and apply IPC immediately without unnecessary examination.
- Notify through the correct Ministry of Health/district outbreak pathway.
- Avoid routine waiting-room flow, routine lab flow, and unnecessary staff exposure.
- Document the concern and actions taken without creating panic.

Reviewer questions:

- Is this scenario realistic for an HC III during an Ebola alert?
- What exact triage questions should be asked?
- What IPC steps should be named for this level of facility?
- What is the correct notification and referral language?
- Are the vitals and screening facts realistic?
